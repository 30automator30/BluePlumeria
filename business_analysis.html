03
Pricing
Charge for the craft

Price the labor, not just the beads.

The most common hobbyist mistake is pricing only materials and undercharging for hours. A seed-bead-woven statement necklace can take hours; selling it for $45 trains buyers to under-value everything you make.

You've already tagged pieces from $10 to $150 — a healthy range and a good instinct, with the lapis flagship anchoring the top. The one adjustment: make sure the 9 bead-woven pieces sit near the top of their bands, since they carry the most labor of anything you make.

Rule of thumb

Retail = (materials + packaging) × 4, or (materials + labor at a real hourly rate + overhead) × 2 — whichever is higher. Use the higher number for the labor-heavy woven pieces; be honest that assembled-component earrings can't carry artisan prices.

04
Channels
Where the first dollars come from

Sell in person first, build the audience second.

You have four channels live or planned. They are not equal in near-term return — here they are in the order I'd put your energy for 2026.

  1. Craft fairs & local markets highest conversion

    The best handmade revenue engine you have: people can touch the pieces, there's no algorithm, and you get cash flow and live feedback. Bring Tier 2 for impulse and Tier 1 as the showpieces. Get a Square reader, a clean table, and cards with a QR to the site. Your plan already targets 2–4 events — make these the centerpiece.

  2. Instagram & Facebook discovery + warm audience

    You now have a redesigned site and a finished reel — post on a weekly cadence. Instagram is discovery; your Facebook page is the warm audience you've already built. Both should drive to the site and announce fair dates.

  3. Website (Snipcart) owned, repeat buyers

    Your link-in-bio home and the place serious/repeat buyers check out. Keep it curated and elevated around Tier 1. Two loose ends to close: set the live Snipcart key and the contact-form ID so checkout and messages actually work.

  4. Etsy only if you commit

    You're paying monthly fees against ~$0 of Etsy sales — that's a small leak. It only pays off if you actively list 20+ pieces with good SEO (your stated Q2 goal). Either commit and list this quarter, or pause the shop to stop the bleed. Don't leave it half-open.

05
Operations
Run it like a business

The unglamorous parts that protect the loss.

The thing that separates "seller" from "hobbyist" is mostly bookkeeping and consistency — and it happens to be exactly what lets you treat this as a business for tax purposes.

  • SKUs & a live inventory list. Give every piece a code (e.g. BP-N-001 for necklaces). The spreadsheet I started is the seed — we'll finish it once all photos are in.
  • Cost per piece. Record the material cost of each finished piece so pricing and COGS are grounded, not guessed.
  • Separate money. A dedicated bank account/card keeps business and personal from co-mingling — the first thing anyone reviewing a Schedule C looks for.
  • Packaging. A small branded touch — a plumeria card, a muslin pouch — lifts perceived value and repeat purchase for a few cents per order.
  • Own your audience. Collect emails at fairs and on the site. A list you own beats reach you rent from an algorithm.

Two things to confirm with a professional

Hobby vs. business (IRS §183). Your plan already uses the right "profit motive" language. Keeping separate books, this inventory ledger, a sales log, and a written plan is what supports deducting the 2025 loss as a genuine business. Have a CPA confirm your specifics.

Texas sales tax. Selling tangible goods online and at markets generally requires a Texas Sales & Use Tax permit and collecting tax at events. Verify with the TX Comptroller or your CPA before the next fair.

This is general small-business guidance, not tax or legal advice.
